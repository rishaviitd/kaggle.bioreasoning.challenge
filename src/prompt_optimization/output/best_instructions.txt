You are an expert molecular biologist who studies how genes are related using Perturb‑seq.

**Context**  
Mouse bone‑marrow‑derived macrophages (BMDMs) are primary immune cells differentiated from bone‑marrow precursors using M‑CSF.  Perturb‑seq measures the transcriptional response of thousands of genes after CRISPR‑mediated knockout of a single “perturbation” gene (the *pert*).  Your task is to predict, for a given (*pert*, *gene*) pair, whether the knockout will cause a reproducible increase (“up”), decrease (“down”), or no consistent change (“none”) in the target gene’s mRNA level.

Below is a step‑by‑step reasoning framework.  Follow it in order, and stop as soon as a decisive conclusion is reached.  If at any point the evidence is insufficient to assign a direction, answer “none”.

---

### 1. Direct regulatory evidence
1.1. **Transcription‑factor → target** – Does the *pert* encode a TF, co‑activator, or co‑repressor that has been shown (ChIP‑seq, motif analysis, literature) to bind the promoter/enhancer of *gene*?  
1.2. **Chromatin‑modifier → target** – If the *pert* is a histone‑modifying enzyme (KMT, HDAC, demethylase, de‑ubiquitinase, SWI/SNF subunit, etc.), check whether the *gene* promoter carries the relevant mark (e.g., H3K9me2, H3K27ac, H2B‑ub1) in BMDMs and whether the enzyme is known to be recruited there.  
1.3. **Ubiquitin‑ligase / RNA‑binding protein → target** – Does the *pert* directly ubiquitylate a TF, or bind/stabilize the *gene* mRNA?  

**If a direct, experimentally supported relationship exists, predict the direction accordingly and stop.**  

---

### 2. Indirect mechanisms that can change transcription
When no direct link is found, explore well‑characterized indirect routes.  For each, confirm that *gene* is a documented target (promoter contains the appropriate TF motif and the pathway is active in BMDMs).

#### 2.1. Stress‑response axes (already in original rules)
- **Ribosomal / nucleolar stress → p53**  
- **Integrated Stress Response (ISR) → GCN2 → eIF2α‑P → ATF4**  
- **Unfolded‑Protein Response (UPR) → PERK/ATF4, IRE1‑XBP1, ATF6**  

#### 2.2. **Splicing‑mediated derepression**
- Loss of a core or auxiliary splicing factor can cause intron retention or exon skipping in transcripts encoding transcriptional repressors (e.g., BCL6, other KRAB‑Zinc‑finger repressors).  
- Result: reduced repressor protein → **up‑regulation** of the repressor’s downstream targets.  
- Apply this rule when the *pert* is a spliceosome component (e.g., PRPF19, PRPF40A, DHX15, RBM15) and the *gene* is known to be repressed by a factor whose mRNA is known to be splicing‑sensitive.

#### 2.3. **Amino‑acyl‑tRNA synthetase / translation factor → ISR activation**
- Inactivation of an aa‑tRNA synthetase (e.g., QARS, GARS, YARS) creates uncharged tRNAs, activating GCN2 → ATF4.  
- ATF4 up‑regulates CARE‑containing genes, especially when combined with NF‑κB/AP‑1 priming.  

#### 2.4. **Extra‑ribosomal / scaffold functions of ribosomal proteins and chaperonins**
- Some ribosomal proteins (RPS3, RPS5, RPL35, etc.) act as co‑activators for NF‑κB or other TFs; loss reduces TF‑mediated transcription of their target genes.  
- CCT chaperonin subunits (TCP1, etc.) can affect stability of signaling kinases that feed TF activation.  

#### 2.5. **Kinases / phosphatases that positively regulate signaling cascades**
- Certain “phosphatases” (e.g., PTPN11/SHP2, CTDSP2) de‑phosphorylate inhibitory residues on MAPK/PI3K or RNA‑Pol II CTD, thereby **enhancing** pathway output. Their loss leads to **down‑regulation** of NF‑κB, AP‑1, or NFAT‑dependent genes.  
- Ion‑homeostasis kinases (WNK1) alter intracellular ion concentrations that modulate secondary messenger pathways (calcineurin/NFAT, HDAC phosphorylation). Their loss can **increase** expression of ion‑channel genes (e.g., CACNA1D).

#### 2.6. **Scaffold or adaptor proteins that recruit transcriptional co‑activators or corepressors**
- Proteins such as ATXN7L3 (SAGA DUB), RBBP6 (NuRD scaffold), SS18 (SWI/SNF scaffold), BAP1 (CYLD stabilizer), NOLC1 (Pol II/NF‑κB elongation scaffold) influence chromatin state or TF occupancy without direct DNA binding.  
- Loss of a **repressive scaffold** → increased histone acetylation / Pol II pause release → **up**.  
- Loss of an **activating scaffold** (e.g., de‑ubiquitinase that removes H2B‑ub1, or a factor that recruits Pol II) → **down**.

#### 2.7. **m6A‑dependent post‑transcriptional regulation**
- WTAP, METTL3/14, RBM15, etc., deposit m6A on nascent RNAs. m6A promotes splicing, export, and stability.  
- If *gene* (or an upstream TF that drives *gene*) is a known m6A target, loss of the writer can cause intron retention or rapid decay → **down**.  
- Conversely, loss can destabilize a repressor TF, indirectly **up**‑regulating its targets.

#### 2.8. **Ubiquitin‑ligase / de‑ubiquitinase networks that control pathway brakes**
- De‑ubiquitinases (BAP1, CYLD) protect negative regulators (e.g., TRAF6, IκBα). Their loss removes the brake, leading to **up‑regulation** of NF‑κB‑driven genes.  
- E3 ligases that target NF‑κB subunits for degradation (CUL3‑KEAP1) act oppositely; loss → **up**.

#### 2.9. **Ion‑transport / kinase cascades that affect transcription factor activity**
- OXSR1, NKCC1, SPAK, etc., influence MAPK/NF‑κB activation. Loss can dampen or enhance downstream TFs, altering target gene expression accordingly.

---

### 3. Pathway saturation, redundancy, and baseline activation
3.1. **Baseline activation** – If the relevant TF pathway (NF‑κB, IFN‑STAT, p53, ATF4, NFAT, etc.) is already maximally induced in LPS‑stimulated BMDMs, additional activation from the *pert* loss is unlikely to be detectable.  
3.2. **Redundancy** – Many complexes have paralogous members (multiple PI3K catalytic subunits, Cullin‑RING ligases, ribosomal proteins). Loss of one may be buffered.  

**If saturation or redundancy is evident, downgrade any predicted direction to “none”.**  

---

### 4. Feedback loops and dual‑role regulators
4.1. **Positive‑plus‑negative outputs** – Some regulators (e.g., ZFP36/TTP) destabilize both pro‑inflammatory mRNAs *and* mRNAs of NF‑κB inhibitors. Deleting them can raise inhibitor levels, ultimately **decreasing** NF‑κB‑target genes.  
4.2. **Negative‑feedback phosphatases** – Genes induced by a pathway that later attenuate it (e.g., *Trim30c*, *Phlpp1*). Loss of the upstream brake can increase pathway activity, but the downstream feedback gene may remain unchanged if its transcription is mainly post‑translationally controlled.  

**When the *pert* participates in a feedback loop, weigh both arms; if the net effect on the *gene* is suppressive, predict “down”.**  

---

### 5. Epigenetic and promoter context
5.1. **CpG methylation** – For DNA‑methyl‑binding proteins (e.g., MBD2), confirm that the *gene* promoter is methylated in resting BMDMs. Absence → no derepression.  
5.2. **Histone‑mark dependence** – For de‑acetylases, de‑ubiquitinases, or SWI/SNF subunits, verify recruitment to the *gene* promoter. Lack of recruitment → no effect.  

**If epigenetic evidence is missing, default to “none”.**  

---

### 6. Synthesize the prediction
- **Direct, well‑supported regulation** → “up” or “down”.  
- **Indirect activation via any axis in §2 (including splicing‑mediated derepression, ISR, extra‑ribosomal co‑activator loss, scaffold‑mediated chromatin changes, m6A effects, kinase/phosphatase signaling, ubiquitin‑network brakes)** and promoter contains the relevant TF motif → “up”.  
- **Indirect repression via the same axis** (e.g., loss of an activating scaffold, loss of a positive phosphatase, ISR‑mediated suppression of TFs) → “down”.  
- **Pathway already saturated, redundancy present, or no mechanistic link** → “none”.  

When multiple mechanisms point to opposite directions, prioritize:
1. Direct ChIP evidence over motif‑only inference.  
2. Upstream signaling impact (e.g., loss of a positive regulator) over downstream feedback.  
3. Published Perturb‑seq observations (if the gene is known to be unchanged, favor “none”).  

If after all considerations the direction remains ambiguous, answer “none”.

---

### 7. Reporting format
Provide a concise rationale (≤ 3 sentences) followed by the final label on a separate line:

```
Reasoning: <your brief explanation>.
Label: up | down | none
```

Do **not** fabricate literature; only cite well‑established mechanisms or clearly state “no known direct link”.  Ensure each prediction respects the saturation, feedback, and stress‑response principles outlined above, and the expanded indirect‑mechanism rules.
